Cr(VI)
Self-Healing Passivation — Maximum Corrosion Protection
Hexavalent chromate creates a gel-like Cr(VI)/Cr(III) mixed-oxide film on zinc or aluminum. The unique self-healing property comes from soluble Cr(VI) species that migrate to scratches and re-passivate exposed metal. This delivers 96–500+ hours of salt-spray protection. Classified as a Group 1 carcinogen (IARC) — restricted by RoHS, ELV, and REACH but still permitted under military and aerospace exemptions.

The Self-Healing Mechanism That No Replacement Has Matched
Hexavalent chromate’s unique advantage is the mobile Cr(VI) reservoir within the gel film. When the coating is scratched or mechanically damaged, soluble Cr(VI) ions migrate through moisture to the damaged area and reduce to Cr(III) oxide, chemically re-sealing the breach. This “self-healing” effect is why hex chrome delivers dramatically higher salt-spray hours than trivalent alternatives — and why aerospace and military specs still call for it when maximum field-proven reliability is required. No drop-in replacement has fully replicated this mechanism.

Did You Know?
Hexavalent chromate conversion was discovered almost by accident in the 1920s when researchers at the Ford Motor Company noticed that chromic acid rinse water left a protective film on zinc-plated parts. The U.S. military adopted it during World War II to protect zinc-plated hardware on aircraft and vehicles deployed in tropical climates. By the 1950s, it became the single most widely used corrosion protection process in the world. Despite decades of effort to replace it, no alternative has matched the self-healing property — the ability of mobile Cr(VI) ions to migrate to a scratch and chemically re-seal it — which is why it remains specified under MIL-DTL-5541 and MIL-DTL-81706 to this day.

⚠ Critical Safety & Regulatory Warning
Cr(VI) is a confirmed human carcinogen (IARC Group 1). Inhaling its mist or dust causes lung cancer, and skin contact causes ulcers and sensitization. Strict engineering controls are required: enclosed tanks with local exhaust ventilation, HEPA filtration for dusts, and continuous air monitoring. Wear a full-face respirator with P100 cartridges whenever controls are insufficient, plus impervious gloves, an apron, and a face shield. The waste is RCRA hazardous (D007) and must be reduced to Cr(III) before discharge. OSHA requires exposure records to be kept for 30+ years. Keep an emergency shower and eyewash within 10 seconds of the station.
⚠ Regulatory Status — Hexavalent Chromium
Hexavalent chromium is a known human carcinogen and one of the most heavily regulated substances in the finishing industry. OSHA sets a strict exposure limit and specific control requirements for it (29 CFR 1910.1026). California is phasing out hexavalent-chromium plating and chromic-acid anodizing under a state air-toxics rule, with deadlines that differ for decorative and functional work, and other states may follow. This poster describes the process as it is still practiced today; it is not a recommendation to run hexavalent chromium. Before you operate or continue this process, confirm what is currently allowed in your location and plan for the transition to trivalent or alternative chemistries where required.
