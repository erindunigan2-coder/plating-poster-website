“The Specification Is the Contract”
If a customer calls out ASTM B633 SC3 Type VI, every word matters: B633 = zinc electroplate, SC3 = Service Condition 3 (12 µm min thickness), Type VI = trivalent chromate passivation. Misreading one element means non-conforming parts.

Other Specification Systems AMS · MIL · ISO

✈
AMS — Aerospace

AMS 2700 — passivation of stainless. AMS 2404 — electroless nickel. AMS QQ-N-290 — nickel plating. AMS 2460 — chromium plating. Widely referenced on aerospace & defense drawings.

⚙
MIL — Military / Defense

MIL-DTL-5541 — chromate conversion on aluminum. MIL-PRF-46010 — hard chrome plating. MIL-DTL-16232 — phosphate coatings. Many cancelled and replaced by AMS or SAE equivalents.

🌐
ISO — International

ISO 2081 — zinc coatings on iron/steel. ISO 4042 — fastener electroplated coatings. ISO 1456 — nickel + chromium on metals. ISO 4527 — autocatalytic (electroless) nickel.

Spec Compliance Do

  • Read the complete, latest revision of every called-out specification
  • Verify service condition classification with the customer before plating
  • Maintain calibrated thickness gauges traceable to NIST standards
  • Document hydrogen embrittlement bake times per spec requirements
  • Confirm chromate type (hex vs. tri) — Type II ≠ Type VI

Common Mistakes Avoid

  • Plating to “nickel plate per spec” without identifying which spec
  • Using an outdated revision — specs are revised and superseded
  • Ignoring service condition — SC1 and SC4 require vastly different thickness
  • Assuming all zinc chromates are interchangeable (hex vs. tri vs. none)
  • Not checking if a MIL spec has been cancelled and replaced by AMS
Why “Service Condition” Matters More Than Thickness Alone
ASTM B633 defines four service conditions that determine the entire plating requirement. SC4 (severe) requires 25 µm zinc minimum plus a supplementary chromate conversion coating for maximum corrosion protection. SC1 (mild) needs only 5 µm for indoor, dry environments. The service condition dictates not just thickness, but also the chromate type, salt spray hours, and acceptance criteria. Specifying the wrong SC means either over-engineering costs or under-engineering performance — both are costly failures.

Spec-Related Failures Common rejection causes

Wrong Chromate Type

Customer specifies Type VI (trivalent) but parts receive Type II (hexavalent yellow). Reject is automatic — especially under RoHS or REACH requirements. Always confirm passivation type before processing.

Thickness Below Minimum

Spec requires measurement at significant surfaces per the standard. Low-current-density areas may pass at the thickest point but fail at the specified test location. Know where to measure.

Wrong ZnNi Composition

ASTM B841 Type I requires 10–15% nickel in the alloy. Drift outside this range is a non-conformance. Monitor alloy composition with XRF per B568 regularly.

H₂ Embrittlement Not Documented

High-strength steel parts require baking within hours of plating per spec (typically 190 °C × 23 hr). If the bake is done but not documented with time, temp, and lot traceability, it is a finding.

⚠ Quality System & Compliance Requirements
Documented processes: Every plating specification requires a written process that matches the spec callout — chemistry, time, temperature, current density. Calibrated instruments: Thickness gauges, pH meters, and temperature controllers must be calibrated and traceable to national standards. Traceability: Lot numbers, bake records, test reports, and material certifications must be retained. Non-conformance creates liability — shipping parts that do not meet the called-out specification is a quality escape that can result in customer rejects, warranty claims, and loss of accreditation (Nadcap, AS9100, IATF 16949).
