Sulfur in Deposit
Keeping sulfur out of the deposit (<0.005% S) is what makes the semi-bright layer more noble than the bright nickel plated over it. That nobility difference — verified by the ASTM B764 STEP test — is why the bright layer corrodes sacrificially and the duplex stack outlasts a single nickel layer of the same thickness. Add sulfur (wrong additive, bright-bath drag-over, or anode residues where a sulfur-free system is specified) and you lose the whole mechanism.

No Class I carrier, no Class II brightener. The single defining additive is the sulfur-free leveler — dose on amp-hours and confirm leveling by a leveling panel or Hull Cell, NOT by brightness.
Cross-bath carryover is a classic duplex failure — bright additive back into this tank adds sulfur; leveler forward dulls the bright bath. Dedicated rinses; never share pumps, cups, or dosing lines between the two nickel baths.
Plain Watts (engineering): matte, unleveled, no additive, ~150–250 HV — not part of the decorative stack; see the Watts Nickel series.

Anode Selection S-nickel vs non-sulfur

Sulfur-Depolarized (S-Rounds)

Dissolves cleanly and efficiently — the general workhorse anode. The trace of anode sulfur affects anode dissolution, not deposit sulfur the way a Class I carrier does — but where the spec demands a genuinely sulfur-free system, be deliberate about it.

Rolled / Electrolytic (Non-Sulfur)

Introduces no sulfur into the system — preferred where the semi-bright layer’s sulfur content is tightly specified. Dissolves less readily and can passivate if anode area or chloride is marginal — keep chloride adequate and anode area generous.

Sulfur-free, defined: for semi-bright, “sulfur-free deposit” means no co-deposited sulfur from organic sulfur-bearing additives. This choice matters more here than for either sibling bath — confirm the anode against the customer’s sulfur-content requirement for the layer.

⚠ Safety
Hot acidic nickel solution (pH 3.5–4.5, up to 150°F) — causes burns and is a dermal sensitizer. Soluble nickel salts are skin sensitizers and nickel compounds are classified as suspected carcinogens. Chronic nickel exposure causes allergic contact dermatitis (“nickel itch”) — cumulative and permanent once sensitized; minimize skin contact at all times. Air agitation generates nickel-containing mist — local exhaust ventilation mandatory; mist eliminators recommended. OSHA PEL for soluble nickel compounds: 1 mg/m³ TWA (as Ni). Rectifier = high amperage — LOTO for all maintenance. Use PP, CPVC, or lined tanks — nickel sulfate corrodes mild steel, and plain PVC is marginal above 140°F. Boric acid: a reproductive-hazard concern — avoid dust inhalation when charging or making additions, wear gloves, wash after handling. PPE: goggles + face shield, elbow-length nitrile chemical gloves, apron, boots, P100 or supplied-air respirator if ventilation is inadequate.
