Typical Oxide Thickness (µm) — Hardcoat Type III Anodize
Hardcoat anodizing grows a dense, hard aluminum oxide layer at near-freezing electrolyte temperatures. At 25–75 µm (up to 100+ on some alloys), the coating reaches 60–70 Rc equivalent hardness — harder than many tool steels. Roughly half the coating grows inward, so dimensional change per side is ~50% of total thickness.

Why Hardcoat Outperforms Conventional Anodize
The secret to hardcoat is temperature. At 28–36°F, the sulfuric acid dissolves the oxide far more slowly than at 68°F, so the coating grows thicker and denser before dissolution catches up. Higher current density (24–36 ASF vs 12–18 for Type II) drives faster growth, and the cold bath keeps the oxide from becoming soft or powdery. The result is an oxide layer 3–5× thicker than Type II with significantly higher hardness. This makes hardcoat the preferred surface treatment for hydraulic cylinders, pistons, valve bodies, and any aluminum part subject to sliding wear or abrasion.

Did You Know?
Hardcoat anodizing requires refrigeration equipment capable of holding the electrolyte at 28–36°F while dissipating the substantial heat generated by 24–36 ASF current flow. A typical 500-gallon hardcoat tank may require 15–25 tons of refrigeration capacity. This energy cost is why hardcoat is 3–5× more expensive per square foot than Type II, but for wear-critical applications the performance justifies the investment many times over.

Type III Hardcoat vs Hard Chrome surface hardening

Hardcoat Anodize Choose When

  • Substrate is aluminum (native oxide growth)
  • Uniform coverage on complex geometries
  • Electrical insulation also needed
  • No environmental issues (no Cr(VI))
  • Integral bond — cannot delaminate

Hard Chrome Choose Instead

  • Substrate is steel, copper, or other metal
  • Surface must be repaired / rebuilt
  • Higher hardness needed (68–72 Rc)
  • Thicker coatings (>100 µm practical)
  • Lower coefficient of friction required
