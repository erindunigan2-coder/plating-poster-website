Nickel Content in Deposit — Critical Alloy Composition
The magic window for zinc-nickel is 12–15% nickel by weight. Below 10% Ni, you lose the corrosion benefit. Above 18% Ni, the deposit becomes brittle and passivation adhesion degrades. Within the 12–15% band, the gamma-phase ZnNi intermetallic forms, delivering extraordinary corrosion resistance that satisfies automotive OEM salt-spray requirements.

Why ZnNi Is the Automotive & Aerospace Standard
Zinc-nickel has become the premium corrosion protection standard because its gamma-phase intermetallic corrodes 3–5× slower than pure zinc. A 10 µm ZnNi deposit with trivalent passivation and sealer routinely delivers 720–1,000+ hours to red rust in salt spray. The alloy also offers a higher service temperature ceiling (~480°F vs ~250°F for pure zinc) before the passivation degrades. Every major automotive OEM now specifies zinc-nickel for underbody fasteners, brake components, and structural brackets.

Did You Know?
Zinc-nickel alloy plating was developed in the 1980s for the European automotive industry when OEMs demanded coatings that could survive 1,000+ hours of salt spray without red corrosion. The secret is the gamma-phase (γ) intermetallic compound that forms at 12–15% nickel: it has a more noble corrosion potential than pure zinc, so it corrodes more slowly while still sacrificially protecting the steel substrate. Today, every major automotive specification worldwide (VW TL 244, GM GMW 3359, Toyota TSH 6650) includes zinc-nickel as the preferred fastener coating.

ACID ZnNi VS ALKALINE ZnNi when to use each

Acid ZnNi Choose When

  • Bright or semi-bright finish needed
  • Die castings or mixed substrates
  • Higher plating speed priority
  • Better leveling on flat parts
  • Lower operating temperature

Alkaline ZnNi Choose Instead

  • Deep recesses or barrel work
  • Superior alloy uniformity (12–15% Ni)
  • High-strength steel (lower HE risk)
  • Complex geometries
  • Tighter alloy composition control
