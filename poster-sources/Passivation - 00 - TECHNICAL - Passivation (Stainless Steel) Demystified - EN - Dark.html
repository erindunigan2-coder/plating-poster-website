The Invisible Shield — Chromium Oxide Passive Layer
Passivation dissolves free iron and iron compounds from the stainless steel surface using nitric acid (HNO₃) or citric acid. This allows the chromium-enriched surface to spontaneously form a stable, self-repairing Cr₂O₃ passive film 1–3 nm thick. Without passivation, machining, grinding, and forming operations leave embedded iron particles that become rust initiation sites on “stainless” steel.

Nitric vs Citric — The Industry Shift
Nitric acid passivation has been the standard for 60+ years, but citric acid is gaining rapid adoption. Citric acid is safer to handle, generates less hazardous waste, passivates faster (4–10 min vs 20–60 min), and is effective across a broader range of stainless grades. ASTM A967 and AMS 2700 now include citric acid methods alongside nitric. The trade-off: citric baths need tighter contamination control (iron drag-in depletes the bath faster) and some legacy specs still mandate nitric only.

Did You Know?
Stainless steel was never meant to be “stainless” without help. Harry Brearley discovered the alloy in Sheffield, England in 1913 while searching for erosion-resistant gun barrel steel. He noticed that his experimental 12.8% chromium alloy did not rust in the cutlery test — but only because the chromium spontaneously formed an invisible Cr₂O₃ oxide layer just 1–5 nm thick. Passivation is the process of restoring and strengthening this natural layer after it has been compromised by machining, welding, or contamination. The shift from nitric acid to citric acid passivation — approved in ASTM A967 since 1999 — was driven largely by the semiconductor and medical device industries, where the reduced environmental impact and elimination of nitric acid fumes made citric passivation both safer and more effective for high-purity applications.

Citric Acid vs Nitric Acid choosing the right method

Citric Acid Modern Standard

  • Safer to handle; less hazardous waste
  • Faster (4–10 min typical)
  • Effective on most SS grades
  • ASTM A967 & AMS 2700 approved

Nitric Acid Legacy Standard

  • 60+ year track record; some specs mandate it
  • Better for heavily contaminated surfaces
  • Stronger oxidizer for stubborn free iron
  • Higher waste treatment cost & handling risk
