#14
A Conversion Coating Transforms the Surface — It Doesn’t Add a Layer on Top
The substrate metal participates directly in the reaction, creating an integral protective film that is chemically bonded to the base material. Unlike electroplated deposits, conversion coatings cannot be peeled away — the coating is the transformed surface itself. This integral bond provides excellent paint adhesion and corrosion resistance even at sub-micron thicknesses.

Key Industry Topics Regulatory & application context

⚠
Hex Chrome Phase-Out

RoHS & REACH restrict hexavalent chromium (Cr⁶⁺) as a carcinogen. Trivalent chromate passivation (Cr₃⁺) is the primary replacement. Hex chromate conversion coatings per MIL-DTL-5541 Class 1A still persist in aerospace under exemption but face tightening timelines.

⚙
Phosphate as Paint Base

Automotive and appliance industries use zinc phosphate as the standard pre-paint treatment. The crystalline phosphate layer (5–25 µm) provides mechanical tooth for paint adhesion and cathodic protection under damaged paint films. Iron phosphate is used for lighter-duty applications.

⚖
Stainless Passivation

Passivation of stainless steel removes free iron and contaminants embedded during machining, grinding, or forming. Restoring the passive Cr₂O₃ film is critical — without it, even 316L will rust. ASTM A967 covers citric and nitric methods; ASTM A380 covers cleaning.

Best Practices Do

  • Control immersion time precisely — conversion coatings continue to grow and can become powdery if over-dipped
  • Seal trivalent chromate passivation with silicate or organic topcoat to match hex chrome NSS performance
  • Verify phosphate crystal size and weight per spec — too coarse reduces paint adhesion
  • Run copper sulfate or ferroxyl tests on passivated stainless to confirm free iron removal
  • Keep trivalent baths free of hexavalent contamination — test regularly with diphenylcarbazide

Common Mistakes Avoid

  • Over-dipping hex chromate — excess film becomes powdery and reduces adhesion of subsequent coatings
  • Dragging hex Cr parts through trivalent bath — Cr⁶⁺ contaminates the entire tank and cannot be removed
  • Skipping sealer on trivalent passivation and expecting hex-level corrosion performance
  • Passivating free-machining stainless grades (303, 416) with standard nitric — sulfur inclusions cause pitting
  • Applying phosphate over inadequately cleaned steel — oil residue prevents crystal nucleation
Why Trivalent Still Trails Hexavalent in Some Applications
Hexavalent chromate coatings are “self-healing” — mobile Cr⁶⁺ ions stored in the gel matrix migrate to scratches and damaged areas, re-passivating the exposed zinc. Trivalent coatings contain only Cr₃⁺, which is insoluble and immobile. This makes trivalent passivation a static barrier with no self-repair mechanism. The performance gap is closed by applying sealers and organic topcoats that act as secondary barriers. In practice, a well-sealed trivalent system routinely achieves 120–200 hours to white rust in NSS testing per ASTM B633, matching or approaching traditional yellow chromate.

Conversion Coating Defects Diagnosis & root cause

Poor Paint Adhesion Over Phosphate

Phosphate crystal weight too low or too high, or oily residue preventing nucleation. Check cleaner effectiveness, phosphate concentration, and accelerator ratio. Target 1.5–4.5 g/m² for zinc phosphate.

Rainbow / Iridescent Chromate (Should Be Clear)

Over-immersion or bath pH too low, causing excess film build. Reduce dip time, check pH (target 1.8–2.2 for most trivalent baths), and verify bath temperature is within spec.

Trivalent Bath Contamination

Hex chrome drag-in from adjacent lines, or metallic impurities (Fe, Cu) from dissolved parts. Test with DPC indicator. Once contaminated with Cr⁶⁺, the bath must typically be discarded.

Passivation Failure on Stainless

Free-machining grades (303, 416) contain sulfur inclusions that resist standard passivation. Use citric acid with extended immersion, or chelant-assisted nitric. Verify with ASTM A967 Practice E (copper sulfate test).

⚠ Safety — Conversion Coating & Passivation Chemicals
Hexavalent chromium (Cr⁶⁺) is a confirmed human carcinogen (OSHA PEL 5 µg/m³) — engineering controls, respiratory protection, and biological monitoring are mandatory for chromating operations. Phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) causes severe burns; wear face shield, acid-resistant gloves, and apron. Nitric acid used in passivation produces toxic NOₓ fumes — local exhaust ventilation required on all passivation tanks. Citric acid is safer but still requires PPE for concentrated solutions. Never mix chromating and passivation chemicals. Maintain SDS access and eyewash stations within 10 seconds of all tanks.
