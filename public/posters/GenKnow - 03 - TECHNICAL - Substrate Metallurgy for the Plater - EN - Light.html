Know Your Substrate — Before You Plate It
Different alloys require different preparation sequences, different plating conditions, and produce different results. A process optimized for low-carbon steel will fail on stainless, aluminum, or zinc die-cast. The metallurgy of the base metal is the single biggest variable you must account for in every plating operation.

Metallurgical Factors That Affect Plating Three critical variables

◆
Grain Size Effects

Fine-grained substrates provide more nucleation sites for deposit growth, producing smoother, more uniform coatings. Coarse grains cause “orange peel” texture in the deposit. Cold-worked surfaces plate differently than annealed — hardness and residual stress affect both adhesion and appearance.

⚙
Alloy Segregation Effects

Alloying elements concentrate at grain boundaries during solidification (coring). Lead, sulfur, silicon, and phosphorus segregation causes preferential etching, uneven activation, and localized adhesion failures. Cast alloys are worse than wrought due to slower cooling and dendritic structures.

🌡
Heat Treatment State

Annealed, quenched, or tempered — the metallurgical condition changes everything. Hardened steel is susceptible to hydrogen embrittlement during cleaning and plating. Precipitation-hardened alloys may have different surface compositions than the bulk. Always know the temper before selecting your process.

Best Practices Do

  • Verify alloy grade from material certs before selecting prep chemistry
  • Adjust acid activation type and strength to match the specific substrate
  • Use a Wood’s nickel strike on all passive alloys (stainless, Inconel, titanium)
  • Perform adhesion bend tests on first articles when changing substrate lots
  • Account for mixed metals on assemblies — galvanic effects change cleaning and plating behavior

Common Mistakes Avoid

  • Running the same prep cycle for every alloy — one size does not fit all
  • Plating directly on stainless or nickel alloys without a strike coat (peeling guaranteed)
  • Ignoring lot-to-lot variation in die-cast porosity and alloy segregation
  • Using HCl activation on aluminum (violent attack — use NaOH etch + desmut instead)
  • Skipping hydrogen embrittlement bake on hardened steel > 40 HRC after plating
Why Zinc Die-Cast Is the Most Difficult Substrate
Zamak alloys (Zamak 3 and 5) combine nearly every challenge a plater can face. Porosity from the casting process traps moisture, oils, and gases that outgas during plating, causing pitting and blistering. Alloy segregation — especially lead and tin at grain boundaries — creates preferential etch sites that undermine adhesion. Outgassing intensifies in warm alkaline cleaners and acid baths, producing gas-track pits in the deposit. The standard approach — alkaline soak, mild acid dip, then a cyanide copper strike — works only if porosity is controlled in the casting. Reject parts with visible surface porosity before they ever reach the plating line.

Substrate-Related Plating Failures Root causes & remedies

Blistering on Die-Cast

Subsurface porosity traps cleaning solutions that outgas during plating or baking. Ensure cyanide copper strike fully seals the surface. Reject castings with visible pinholes — no amount of process adjustment compensates for poor castings.

Peeling on Stainless Steel

Passive Cr₂O₃ film not fully broken before plating. Use anodic activation in H₂SO₄ followed by a Wood’s nickel strike (HCl + NiCl₂, high current density). Never skip the strike — direct plating on passivated stainless always fails.

Hydrogen Embrittlement on High-Strength Steel

Atomic hydrogen absorbed during acid cleaning and cathodic plating migrates to grain boundaries and causes delayed brittle fracture. Bake at 190–220 °C within 4 hours of plating per ASTM B849/B850. Critical for > 40 HRC and all spring steels.

Galvanic Issues on Mixed-Metal Assemblies

Different metals on the same rack create galvanic cells in cleaning and plating baths. Noble metals steal current from active ones, causing skip plating on less noble substrates and over-plating on copper or stainless components. Rack like metals together.

⚠ Safety — Acid Activation on Different Substrates
HCl activation on steel produces hydrogen gas — ensure ventilation. HF-bearing solutions for stainless and titanium are acutely toxic — calcium gluconate gel must be at the station. NaOH/KOH etches for aluminum generate intense heat and hydrogen — control temperature and never add water to concentrated caustic. Cyanide copper strikes for die-cast are lethal if acid is dragged in — maintain rinse integrity and pH monitoring upstream. Always match PPE to the specific acid: face shield and acid-rated gloves minimum; supplied-air respirator for HF work.
