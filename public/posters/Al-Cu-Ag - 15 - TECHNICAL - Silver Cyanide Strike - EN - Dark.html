Thin Silver Seed Layer Over Copper — Hot Entry Required

HOT ENTRY
Current ON Before Parts Enter Bath
Apply current to the rack before immersing parts. If parts sit in the silver cyanide strike without current, dissolved silver ions displace copper from the surface (immersion/cementation), forming a loose, non-adherent silver layer before electrodeposited silver can protect it. High free cyanide is what suppresses this displacement — but it cannot prevent it on a bare, uncurrented surface. Hot entry is mandatory.

Why the Strike Bath Is Low-Metal / High-Cyanide
The strike bath maintains a free-cyanide-to-silver-metal ratio of approximately 15–25:1 by weight. This extreme ratio serves a specific purpose: it complexes silver so tightly that immersion (chemical displacement) deposition is suppressed. Without this ratio, silver ions would spontaneously displace copper from the surface through an uncontrolled galvanic reaction, producing a powdery non-adherent deposit. By forcing deposition to occur only through applied current (electrodeposition), the silver deposits uniformly and bonds strongly to the copper interlayer.

Anode Selection

Stainless steel (inert) anodes are used in the strike bath. Silver anodes would dissolve and raise the metal concentration, defeating the low-metal design. With inert anodes, silver is replenished only by controlled AgCN additions, maintaining the critical high free-cyanide-to-metal ratio.

Monitor Ag metal concentration. Replenish AgCN as silver depletes through plating.

Carbonate Control

Potassium carbonate accumulates from CO₂ absorption and cyanide oxidation. Keep below 15 g/L. High carbonate reduces conductivity and can produce rough deposits. Remove by cooling crystallization if levels exceed limits. Silver cyanide baths use potassium salts (not sodium) for better solubility.

K₂CO₃ <15 g/L — test weekly in production environments.

Troubleshooting Silver strike issues

Poor Adhesion / Peeling Silver

Parts entered bath without current (cold entry), allowing chemical dissolution of the copper surface. Verify hot entry procedure. Also check free KCN — if too low, immersion deposition can occur even with current applied.

Dark or Discolored Deposit

Current density too high or bath contaminated. Reduce current. Check AgCN and free KCN levels. Carbon treat (2–5 g/L activated carbon) if organic contamination suspected.

No Deposit / Thin Coverage

Silver metal depleted or poor electrical contact. Test AgCN level and replenish. Check rack contacts — poor connections prevent current flow to affected parts.

Copper Surface Etched / Attacked

Parts immersed without adequate current, letting silver ions displace copper (immersion/cementation) before electrodeposition covers the surface. Confirm hot entry and that CD is within range across all parts. Reduce dwell time before current is fully established.

⚠ Safety
CYANIDE BATH — KCN is lethal by ingestion, inhalation, and skin absorption. This is the third cyanide bath on this line. NEVER mix cyanide solutions with acid — this generates deadly hydrogen cyanide (HCN) gas. Maintain bath pH >10 at all times. Operate under slot ventilation at minimum 100 fpm face velocity. HCN monitors must be installed and calibrated. Cyanide antidote kit must be accessible within 30 seconds. Wear chemical-resistant gloves, face shield, and apron. Know the location of the emergency shower and eyewash station.
